# Data gaps — products we can't fetch automatically (and the way out)

This is the honest inventory of what our automation **does not** cover, country by
country, and what to do about each. Read it alongside [MARKET_DATA.md](MARKET_DATA.md)
(what *is* automated).

## Real-only serving (September 2026)

The app now serves **only sourced rates**. `product_rates` and
`remittance_charges` carry `is_indicative`, but nothing outside the admin portal
ever read it — so a figure generated by [seed-rates.mjs](scripts/seed-rates.mjs)
reached users as a benchmark with a named bank attached, indistinguishable from a
real Bank of Thailand rate. (This is how "MUFG Bank, 7.69%" became Singapore's
best personal loan: MUFG was tagged `japanese`, a category the generator prices
0.35pp below base. There was no source, and MUFG does not offer retail personal
loans in Singapore.)

[ratesRepo.js](server/ratesRepo.js) now filters both pools to
`is_indicative = false`, and every benchmark response carries
`dataQuality: 'real' | 'indicative' | 'mixed' | null`. A pool with no sourced
rows returns *"No benchmark data for this selection yet."* rather than an
invention.

Set `RATES_REAL_ONLY=false` to serve indicative rows again during the transition —
they are then labelled `indicative` in the response, never unmarked.

**Coverage as of 12 Sep 2026: 15 of 50 country×product pools serve real data**
— 🇹🇭 Thailand (5 products, BOT), 🇲🇾 Malaysia (housing loan, BNM; fixed
deposit, 5 banks), 🇸🇬 Singapore (fixed deposit, 10 banks; personal loan, 7),
🇮🇳 India (fixed deposit, 10 banks; housing loan, 9; personal loan, 9; vehicle
loan, 6), 🇮🇩 Indonesia (housing and personal loans, 5 banks each). The loan pools carry ranges and floors — see the
next section. Everything else returns "no data yet" — see the scraped-sources
section for what is reachable and what is not.

## Loans published as ranges and floors (11 Sep 2026)

Loan pricing in Singapore, India and Indonesia is rarely one number. A bank
prints either a **range** ("8.00% – 9.40%", by loan slab or credit score) or a
**floor** ("from 7.50%", "7.60% p.a. onwards"). The pool had no way to say
which, so the choice was to invent a single figure or leave the product empty.

**The basis, decided 11 Sep 2026:** a range is stored as its **midpoint** and
shown as **"~8.70%"**; a floor is stored as published and shown as
**"from 7.50%"**. Nothing is invented — the basis is whatever the lender
printed, and a range keeps both ends so the midpoint is always traceable.

How it is built:

- **Storage** — [supabase/rate_basis.sql](supabase/rate_basis.sql) adds
  `rate_basis` (`exact` | `range` | `from`), `rate_low` and `rate_high` to
  `product_rates`, with a check that a range's `rate` *is* its midpoint. `rate`
  keeps carrying the comparable number, so every existing reader still works.
- **Parsing** — [scripts/lib/rate-text.mjs](scripts/lib/rate-text.mjs) turns a
  published string into `{ basis, value, low, high }`, deterministically, and
  refuses anything it cannot read. Tenor words never masquerade as a range
  ("1 year to 2 years: 6.25%" is exact 6.25) and a footnote star is punctuation
  ("7.60%\* p.a. onwards" is a floor of 7.60). Both the scraper and the CSV
  importer run every rate through it, so a hand-filled cell may say
  "8.00-9.50" or "from 7.5".
- **Serving** — every benchmark response now carries `benchmarkBasis` (how the
  *best* rate was published), `benchmarkLow` / `benchmarkHigh` for a range, and
  `approxPool` (true when any rate in the pool is a midpoint or a floor, so the
  median and market-typical are approximate too).
- **Screen** — the calculator prints the best rate as "~8.70%" or "from 7.50%",
  puts "~" on the market-typical figure when the pool is approximate, and adds
  one line saying what the mark means.

**Live since 11 Sep 2026 (evening).** `rate_basis.sql` has been run and the
four pools — India housing, personal and vehicle loans, Singapore personal
loans — are loaded and refresh nightly with everything else. (If the migration
is ever missing on another database, the importer loads the exact-only pools
in a file and **holds back** any pool with a range or a floor — checked before
a single row is deleted — and exits non-zero so the nightly job shows red.)

> ⚠️ **One thing the basis rule does to a ranking.** A bank that publishes a
> wide range is represented by its midpoint; a bank that publishes only its
> floor keeps the floor. Side by side, the floor always looks cheaper — ICICI's
> "8.50% – 9.40%" becomes ~8.95% while Union Bank's "from 7.15%" stays 7.15%,
> although ICICI's best-profile borrowers pay 8.50%. The "~" and "from" marks
> are there precisely so a reader can see this; the pool is honest, but "best
> of N" is now "best published floor or midpoint of N", and the tip under the
> result should say so.

**Basis per pool** (each figure is read from the bank's *own* page; the Indian
comparison sites were checked and rejected — Paisabazaar's car-loan table is
dated 2019, and both it and BankBazaar disagree with the banks on several rows):

- 🇮🇳 **Housing loan** — the bank's standard floating home-loan rate for a
  salaried borrower: the published range where the bank prints one (ICICI by
  loan slab, Axis by credit score), else its advertised floor.
- 🇮🇳 **Personal loan** — the published effective-rate range (SBI, ICICI, HSBC),
  else the advertised floor.
- 🇮🇳 **Vehicle loan** — new-car loan: SBI's published range, else the floor.
- 🇸🇬 **Personal loan** — the lender's lowest advertised **effective** rate
  (EIR). Every Singapore bank prints a flat rate ("1.48% p.a.") beside an EIR
  ("3.22% p.a."); the flat figure is not comparable to a reducing-balance rate,
  the EIR is. MoneySmart's comparison table was checked and *not* used: it
  disagrees with DBS (2.84 vs the bank's 3.22) and shows an exclusive promo for
  GXS (1.84 vs the bank's 5.45).

## Loading real rates — the fast path

`npm run import:template -- SG` writes `data/rate-templates/SG.csv` pre-filled
with the country, product and provider roster; fill only the `rate` column from
each bank's published rate sheet, then:

```
npm run import:pool:dry -- data/rate-templates/SG.csv   # validate
npm run import:pool     -- data/rate-templates/SG.csv   # write as is_indicative = false
```

Blank rows are skipped, so a partially filled file loads fine — but by default
the importer **replaces** the pool for each (country, product) it contains, so
either fill every bank you intend to keep or pass `--merge`, which replaces
only the banks named in the file and leaves the rest of the pool alone. The
nightly job imports with `--merge`, so a hand-entered bank survives it; remove
a bank through the admin portal, not by leaving it out of a file. A brief for
extracting the remaining rates by hand, with the rules that make each row
checkable, is in
[data/rate-templates/EXTRACTION_PROMPT.md](data/rate-templates/EXTRACTION_PROMPT.md).

## Scraped sources (`npm run scrape:pool`) — runs nightly

[scrape-pool.mjs](scripts/scrape-pool.mjs) pulls per-bank rates from published
pages. Each source declares an exact table (header pattern), row (label pattern)
and column index — and, for cells that carry two rates in prose ("1.35% branch /
1.40% online"), the words that pick one. If any selector stops matching, the
source **fails loudly and is skipped**, and a (country, product) pool is written
only when *every* source feeding it parsed, so a bank redesign can never shrink
a pool from an unattended run — and a broken loan source does not hold back that
night's deposit refresh. Pages that draw their tables in JavaScript are rendered
in headless Chromium (`render: true`). Rates a bank prints in a sentence rather
than a table are read with a `text` pattern that must name the product in the
bank's own words; SBI's site-wide rates panel, where product and rate are tied
only by markup, uses an `html` pattern spanning from the product's label to its
figure. It printed the matched row or phrase as evidence for all 53 values
below.

**Why deterministic parsing and not a model:** asked to read BCA's SBDK table, a
model returned KPR 6.82 / Non-KPR 9.06. The true columns are KPR **9.06** /
Non-KPR **8.37** — it had shifted by two. Use `npm run inspect:page <url>` to see
a page's tables with their headings, then count.

## First, two data layers — don't confuse them

Our benchmark uses two different kinds of number, and automation only reaches one
of them today:

| Layer | What it is | Feeds | Automated today? |
|-------|-----------|-------|------------------|
| **Benchmark pool** | The set of **per-bank** rates a user's rate is compared against (the "best of N providers"). | `product_rates` in Supabase, seeded by [seed-rates.mjs](scripts/seed-rates.mjs). | **No** — curated, **except 🇹🇭 Thailand** (BOT per-bank, live via [bot-pool.mjs](scripts/bot-pool.mjs)) and **🇲🇾 Malaysia** (BNM housing loan, live via [bnm-pool.mjs](scripts/bnm-pool.mjs)). |
| **Market reference** | A single **aggregate** "📊 market typical" line shown beside the result. | `market_rates`, written by [market-rates.mjs](scripts/market-rates.mjs). | **Partly** — central-bank aggregates only (SG/MY/TH). |

So even for a product marked "automated" below, automation usually feeds only the
*reference line* — the actual per-bank **pool** it scores against is still curated
everywhere but Malaysia. Every gap in this file is really a gap in the pool.

## Per-bank remittance — built, waiting on fees (11 Sep 2026)

A bank's cost to send money abroad is its **FX spread** (telegraphic-transfer
selling rate against mid) plus **flat fees**. The pool now compares both
properly: [scrape-remit.mjs](scripts/scrape-remit.mjs) reads each bank's TT
buy/sell table and measures the spread against the bank's *own* mid
((sell + buy) / 2 — timing-free, unlike the once-a-day ECB fix), and
[server/benchmark.js](server/benchmark.js) `remittanceCostPct` sizes each
bank's flat fees on the amount the customer actually sent, for the corridor
the customer named (a S$20 fee is 2% of S$1,000 and 0.2% of S$10,000; a USD
spread says nothing about an INR transfer). [import-remit.mjs](scripts/import-remit.mjs)
merges rows by (bank, direction, counterparty) and looks bank ids up in
`banks`, never inventing one.

**Spread sources that parse today** (counter TT rate, USD): Bank Mandiri,
Bank Central Asia, OCBC Indonesia, Bangkok Bank. Their e-rates (Mandiri
"Special Rate", BCA "e-Rate") are tighter but app-only with limits, so the
counter rate is the basis.

**Why nothing is loaded yet:** not one of the four banks publishes its
outward-TT fee on a page a machine can read tonight — the tariffs are PDFs
or behind cookie walls. A spread-only row would understate every bank's cost,
so the scraper refuses to write until a person has entered the fee with its
URL and date; [remit-fees-TODO.md](data/rate-templates/remit-fees-TODO.md)
lists exactly what to read where. Singapore, Malaysia, India and the other
Thai banks publish no FX table a browser can read either; their spreads and
fees are a tariff-reading job too, loadable with `import:remit`.

## Cross-cutting: products with no public source *anywhere*

Some products are unpublished by design in **every** market — no regulator or
aggregator carries them, because they're negotiated per deal:

| Product | Why no source exists | Only real way out |
|---------|---------------------|-------------------|
| Short-term / trade finance (funded) | Priced per RFQ against tenor, counterparty risk, collateral. | **W6** advisor deal book → **W7** ICC Trade Register. |
| Unfunded facility (L/C, BG, SBLC) | Commissions are bilateral, confidential. | **W6** → **W7**. |
| SME overdraft **spread** | Board rate is public-ish; the negotiated spread that matters is not. | **W6**. |
| Cross-border **per-bank margin** | The visible fee ≠ the real cost; margin is baked into the FX rate. | **W7** Wise Comparison API. |

For these, the **advisor deal book (W6)** is the primary strategy — advisors log the
real negotiated quotes and closed deals, which is the only place this pricing exists.
A licensed feed (**W7**, e.g. ICC Trade Register / Wise) covers what advisors can't.
